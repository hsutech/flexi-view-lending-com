Best Commercial Real Estate Bridge Lenders: 7 Options to Compare

Commercial bridge debt is short-term capital for a specific transition: acquisition, lease-up, renovation, recapitalization, payoff, refinance or sale. The lender fit changes with the property and the exit.

Quick answer

The best commercial real estate bridge lender is the one whose credit box matches the actual transition. A $40 million industrial bridge, a value-add apartment acquisition, an affordable housing preservation deal, a California mixed-use refinance and a small-balance retail payoff can point to different lenders, even when each loan is called a bridge loan.

Current Google US results for this keyword are BOFU and comparison-driven. The relevant SERP includes lender listicles such as YieldStack, NerdWallet and Fit Small Business, direct product pages from commercial bridge lenders, and California-specific lender pages. Most ranking pages use a table of lender options, a short explanation of bridge loan use cases, author or reviewer attribution, FAQs, and terms such as loan amount, LTV or LTC, closing speed, asset classes and exit strategy.

This guide follows that intent but avoids a universal "best" claim. Commercial bridge loans are too deal-specific for that. A good shortlist should separate institutional lenders, private debt funds, affordable multifamily specialists, local California private lenders and brokered capital. It should also force questions that many listicles underplay: debt yield at exit, recourse, reserves, third-party reports, extension rights, licensing, servicing and whether the lender funds from its own balance sheet or shops the file after issuing an indication.

Seven commercial real estate bridge lender options

1. Ready Capital for larger national CRE bridge requests

Ready Capital publishes a dedicated commercial bridge program for acquisition, refinance and recapitalization. Its public page separates core assets such as industrial, self-storage and essential retail from non-core assets such as office, non-essential retail and hospitality, with different LTC limits. It also lists interest-only amortization, SOFR-based floating rates, future advances for capital expenditures and reserves, and non-recourse language with standard carve-outs.

That makes Ready Capital a relevant comparison point for sponsors with larger commercial assets and a defined business plan. The diligence question is not simply "Can they lend?" Ask which program the property fits, whether the future-advance budget is fully underwritten, how exit and extension fees work, what minimum interest applies, and whether the takeout lender would underwrite the stabilized property in the same way.

2. iBorrow for transitional assets that need private CRE capital

iBorrow markets nationwide commercial real estate bridge loans for acquisitions, refinancing, recapitalizations and value-add plans. Its current bridge page lists office, industrial, retail, hospitality, multifamily, self-storage and student housing as property types, and states that the program excludes raw land and ground-up construction. It also publishes a two-to-four-week closing expectation, interest-only payments, first-trust-deed collateral, non-recourse language subject to carve-outs and in-house servicing.

This can fit sponsors who need a lender comfortable with transitional income, property improvements or a recapitalization that will not wait for bank timing. It may be less relevant for small-balance deals because the published loan-size floor is high. Before relying on speed, confirm title status, third-party reports, environmental needs, whether yield maintenance applies, and who has final credit authority.

3. AVANA Capital for bridge-to-permanent planning

AVANA Capital publishes commercial real estate bridge loans for acquisition, refinance and repositioning toward stabilization. Its public page lists short-term, often interest-only financing, SOFR-based pricing ranges, loan amounts in the mid-market range, up to three-year terms, and property categories that include hospitality, industrial, multifamily, retail, self-storage and warehouse.

AVANA is worth comparing when the exit is central to the request. A commercial bridge loan should not be treated as permanent debt with a shorter maturity. If the plan is to refinance into permanent commercial debt, SBA financing or another takeout, ask what permanent-loan assumptions the lender is using, what DSCR or debt yield the property must hit, whether recourse changes after stabilization and what happens if rates move before the exit.

4. Walker & Dunlop Affordable Bridge Capital for affordable multifamily

Walker & Dunlop Affordable Bridge Capital is more specialized than a general commercial bridge lender. The product is aimed at affordable multifamily and seniors housing, with use cases such as acquisition, rehabilitation, preservation and recapitalization while permanent financing is being arranged. Public materials describe interest-only short-term first mortgage bridge loans with loan sizes in the institutional range.

This option is relevant when the property is not just "multifamily" but affordable housing with LIHTC, Section 8, bond or preservation timing. Those deals often require a lender that understands regulatory restrictions, subsidy contracts, rehabilitation schedules and permanent agency or government-affordable execution. A market-rate value-add apartment deal may belong elsewhere.

5. Lone Oak Fund for California first-trust-deed bridge loans

Lone Oak Fund positions itself as a California bridge lender for first trust deeds on California investment real estate. Its public lending parameters include multifamily, industrial, office, retail, mixed-use and non-owner-occupied residential properties. It also publishes California-only scope, first-trust-deed positioning, acquisition, refinance and cash-out uses, LTV differences by multifamily versus other property types, and a no-prepayment-penalty message.

Lone Oak is a useful comparison point for California assets where local valuation and execution certainty matter. Sponsors should still confirm current rates, evaluation fees, extension terms, whether a personal guaranty is required for the specific file, and what documentation turns an initial discussion into a fundable commitment.

6. North Coast Financial for smaller California CRE bridge loans

North Coast Financial publishes a California commercial bridge loan page for retail, office, mixed-use, multifamily and other commercial property. The page explains bridge financing as short-term capital used to acquire property or create time for long-term financing, and it lists a smaller loan range than institutional CRE lenders.

That makes North Coast more relevant for California borrowers whose request is too small, too fast or too local for a large national bridge desk. Small-balance bridge lending can be practical, but terms vary by collateral and lien position. Confirm whether the loan is first or second lien, how valuation is handled, whether the transaction is owner-occupied or business purpose, and how California DRE or NMLS information applies to the proposed loan.

7. California Bridge Lenders for direct California private bridge requests

California Bridge Lenders publishes direct bridge loan parameters for commercial and non-owner-occupied residential properties, including multifamily, light industrial, special use, strip malls, vacant buildings, hotels, motels, mixed use, office, self-storage and residential rental portfolios. Its public page lists California office information and a California BRE number.

This kind of local private lender can be appropriate when the collateral is specific to California and the borrower needs a short-term first look before a conventional lender can move. The tradeoff is that published terms are only a starting point. Ask whether the stated close speed depends on clean title, how no-appraisal language is handled, whether credit and liquidity still affect approval, and whether the person quoting the file is the lender or a broker.

How to compare commercial real estate bridge lenders

Start by naming the actual bridge. Is the property being acquired quickly, renovated, leased up, recapitalized, rescued from a maturity, repositioned after distress or carried until sale? A lender that fits a stabilized cash-out refinance may not fit a vacant office repositioning or an affordable multifamily preservation timeline.

Then compare the underwriting metrics that control proceeds. CRE bridge lenders may discuss LTV, LTC, loan-to-stabilized value, debt yield, DSCR at exit, sponsor liquidity, guarantor strength, reserves, replacement reserves, interest reserves and capex holdbacks. The lower loan amount often comes from the strictest test, not the most attractive number in a marketing table.

Next, model total cost through the expected payoff date. Interest rate is only one line. Points, exit fees, extension fees, minimum interest, legal fees, third-party reports, fund-control costs, servicing fees, unused-line treatment and prepayment language can change the outcome. For value-add deals, also model a delayed stabilization scenario and one extension.

Finally, pressure-test the takeout. A credible exit is more than "refinance later." Multifamily may need sustained occupancy and NOI before agency debt works. Hospitality may need trailing cash flow and brand compliance. Office may need lease execution. Retail may need tenant rollover resolved. Industrial may need environmental comfort and tenant credit. The bridge lender should be able to explain the exit assumptions before closing.

  • Ask whether the lender funds directly, brokers the file or uses a participation structure.
  • Confirm recourse, non-recourse carve-outs and any completion or payment guarantees.
  • Get reserves, future advances and draw conditions in writing.
  • Compare extension rights before assuming the loan can run longer.
  • Verify servicing contact and payoff procedures before signing.

California diligence before choosing a bridge lender

California licensing review depends on the borrower, collateral, loan purpose, lien position, broker role and exemptions. The California Financing Law resources from the DFPI explain that the law licenses and regulates finance lenders and brokers making or brokering consumer and commercial loans, except as specified. Some real estate-secured transactions may involve a DRE broker, a California Financing Law license, an NMLS-related residential framework, an exemption or a business-purpose structure.

The practical step is to verify the parties, not just the website. Use the California Department of Real Estate public license lookup where applicable, review NMLS information when relevant, and ask the lender or broker to identify the legal entity making or arranging the loan. If an intermediary is involved, ask who gets paid, when fees become nonrefundable, who issues the final loan documents and who services the loan after funding.

That framing can be useful before a lender sees the file, but borrowers should still verify current written terms, licensing or exemption status, and advisor review for legal, tax and financial questions.

FAQs

Who is the best commercial real estate bridge lender?

There is no universal best lender. The right match depends on asset class, loan size, market, leverage, sponsor experience, collateral condition, timing, recourse, reserves and the exit plan.

How are CRE bridge lenders different from residential bridge lenders?

CRE bridge lenders focus on commercial and income-producing collateral such as multifamily, industrial, office, retail, hospitality, self-storage and mixed-use property. Their underwriting usually gives more weight to NOI, lease-up, debt yield, business plan and permanent takeout.

What documents should I prepare before requesting terms?

Prepare the property address, purchase contract or payoff statement, current rent roll, trailing operating statements, sponsor bio, entity documents, budget, capex plan, leases, insurance, title information, environmental reports if available and a clear sale or refinance exit.

What should I compare besides rate?

Compare proceeds, points, extension fees, exit fees, minimum interest, reserve requirements, recourse, draw process, third-party report timing, servicing, prepayment terms and the lender's track record with the same property type.

Can a commercial bridge loan be used for value-add multifamily?

Yes. Value-add multifamily is one of the common bridge use cases. The loan may fund acquisition and part of the renovation or lease-up plan, then exit through agency, bank, CMBS, DSCR-style permanent debt or sale after stabilization.

Is a bridge loan the same as hard money?

They overlap, but they are not identical. Bridge describes short-term capital used between two financing events. Hard money often describes private, asset-based lending more broadly, including some bridge loans, rehab loans, cash-out loans and special-situation loans.

Final thought

A commercial real estate bridge lender should be selected around the business plan, not a headline quote. The best process is to prepare one clean package, compare lenders by the same assumptions and reject any term sheet that does not explain the exit. When the facts are organized, the application page is the simplest way to start a practical review.
